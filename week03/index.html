Section 1 — Data Structures, Formats & Storage

Start with the shape of the data. Structure determines how information can be stored, validated, queried, integrated, and analyzed.

Structured

Rows and columns with a predefined schema. Examples include sales transactions, patient demographics, payroll records, and inventory levels. Relational SQL databases and spreadsheets handle this form well.

Semi-structured

Keys, tags, or nested elements provide organization without a rigid table. JSON, XML, log files, and many API responses fit here.

Unstructured

Free-form content such as emails, social posts, customer comments, clinician narratives, images, audio, and video. These sources often contain rich context but need additional processing.

SQL vs. NoSQL

Use SQL when relationships, transaction accuracy, stable schemas, and auditability matter. Use NoSQL when schema flexibility, high velocity, or large-scale document/key-value data is central.

Representation formats

CSV is simple tabular text. JSON uses keys, values, arrays, and nested objects. XML uses named tags and nested elements. A manager should recognize the format because it affects validation, integration, and tooling.

Managerial question: Before choosing a tool, ask: What kind of data is this, what structure does it have, and what questions do we need it to answer?

Section 2 — Data Sources, Quality & Enterprise Records

Managers combine internal and external sources. The challenge is not only obtaining data, but knowing which system owns the authoritative value and whether the record is fit for analysis.

Internal sources

ERP finance and operations records, CRM customer interactions, SCM supplier and logistics data, EHR encounters, billing, HR, and operational process metrics.

External sources

Market research, competitors, Census, CDC/CMS, economic indicators, social media, payer data, epidemiology, and other public or partner data.

ERP · CRM · SCM

CRM captures demand and customer interaction. SCM coordinates supply and fulfillment. ERP records and controls the financial and operational impact. Analytics becomes stronger when handoffs between these systems reconcile.

Database · Spreadsheet · API

Databases support persistent multi-user records; spreadsheets support flexible ad hoc analysis and modeling; APIs connect systems programmatically and can provide near-real-time data.

Record discipline

Define the system of record, the grain of each dataset, and the primary key. Validate accuracy, completeness, consistency, permitted values, and missing fields before analysis.

Why this matters: A complete dataset can still be wrong. A clean-looking dashboard can still double-count if two sources use different grains or keys.

Section 3 — ETL, Warehousing & Analytical Infrastructure

Operational systems run the organization; analytical infrastructure combines their outputs into consistent, reusable information for decision-making.

ExtractPull the needed records from ERP, CRM, EHR, APIs, databases, or files.
TransformClean, standardize, join, validate, and calculate derived measures.
LoadPlace analysis-ready data into a warehouse, lake, or analytical platform.
KPI LayerDefine shared measures once so teams calculate them consistently.
DashboardServe trends, targets, exceptions, and drill-downs to managers.
DecisionUse the evidence to choose an action.
MonitorMeasure the result and repeat the loop.

Warehouse, lineage, dictionary, and quality

A data warehouse creates an integrated historical source for analysis. Lineage traces where a metric came from and what transformations produced it. A data dictionary defines fields, types, valid values, owners, and meaning. Data quality includes accuracy, completeness, consistency, validity, uniqueness, and timeliness.

Big Data adds the challenges of Volume, Velocity, and Variety. The right architecture depends on the decision speed and the formats involved.

Managerial habit: Never accept a KPI without knowing its definition, denominator, source, refresh time, and transformation path.

Section 4 — Cross-Source Evidence & Case Analysis

Week 3 moves from architecture to analysis: combine numerical operational evidence with human feedback so that one source can validate, explain, or challenge another.

Business case — TPay

Customer reviews are unstructured. Transaction logs are semi-structured. Classify themes and sentiment, calculate transaction success/failure patterns, and ask whether complaint themes align with recorded failures.

Healthcare case — Supplier & Mobile Pay

Calculate supplier cycle times, assess efficiency and reliability, then interpret technical log evidence such as successful authorization followed by failed internal synchronization.

What counts as evidence?

Use transaction IDs, timestamps, status codes, error codes, cycle times, counts, rates, and recurring themes. Distinguish a symptom (for example, a failed transaction) from a root cause (for example, a synchronization timeout or process breakdown).

For supplier performance, do not judge on averages alone. Speed, consistency, and adequate sample size all matter. For customer feedback, text can explain impact that system logs cannot capture.

Recommendation standard: State the action, cite the evidence, explain the expected impact, and identify the metric that will verify whether the action worked.

Cycle time measures elapsed time from the start of a process to completion. Longer cycle times may signal delay, complexity, or process risk.

Section 5 — Governance, Dashboards & Closed-Loop Management

The analytical workflow ends with management action, governance, and measurement—not with a chart.

Refresh cadence

Match refresh frequency to the decision. Near-real-time operations may need fresh signals; quarterly strategy may not.

Privacy & least privilege

PII and PHI require purpose limitation, minimum necessary access, security controls, and often de-identification.

Drill-down & exceptions

Top-line averages can hide a failing facility, supplier, service, segment, or time period. Managers need exceptions and paths to investigate them.

Leading vs. lagging indicators

Lagging KPIs summarize outcomes already realized; leading indicators can provide early signals of emerging risk or future outcomes.

Closed-loop management

Use a repeatable cycle: detect → diagnose → act → verify → standardize or adjust. A dashboard should surface decision-relevant KPIs, targets, trends, exceptions, and drill-down paths rather than display every available number.

End-of-week standard: You should be able to trace a managerial conclusion from source system → structure → transformation → KPI → evidence → action → monitored result.
