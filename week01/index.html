SECTION 1 OF 10

Lecture foundation: how managers turn data into decisions

Your Week 1 lecture establishes the concepts that sit underneath the spreadsheet model. Read these first, then use the order-quantity exercise to apply them.

BUSINESS ANALYTICS

Business Analytics is the methodical exploration of organizational data to produce actionable insight. The goal is not a chart; the goal is a better decision.

BI VS. ANALYTICS

Business Intelligence (BI) asks what happened. Business Analytics goes further: why did it happen, what will happen next, and what should we do?

The three pillars

DESCRIPTIVE

What happened?
PivotTables, summary statistics, dashboards.

PREDICTIVE

What will happen?
Regression, forecasting, machine learning.

PRESCRIPTIVE

What should we do?
Optimization, simulation, decision models.

DDDM: Question → Collection → Analysis → Interpretation → Action

The Data-Driven Decision-Making framework begins with a managerial question, gathers relevant evidence, analyzes it, translates results into a plain-language “so what,” then implements and measures the action.

BADIR: Business Objective → Analytics Approach → Data → Insights → Results

BADIR reinforces the same discipline: define the business objective first, choose the analytical approach, collect only relevant data, generate decision-ready insight, then act and measure results.

Why the order-quantity model belongs here

The muffin/vaccine assignment is a prescriptive decision model under uncertainty. Demand is uncertain, quantity is controllable, overage and underage create competing costs, and the spreadsheet helps management choose the best quantity within real-world constraints.

Why muffins behave like hospital beds

Morning Brew Café throws out unsold muffins at the end of the day, and turns away customers when it runs out. That combination — a product that expires and a cost for not having enough of it — is called perishable inventory. It shows up anywhere a manager has to commit to a quantity before knowing exactly how much will be needed: flu doses ordered before flu season, nurses scheduled before patients arrive, hotel rooms held before the night's bookings are final.

Click each decision below to check whether it follows the same logic as the muffin problem.

CLASSIFY THE DECISION0 / 4 checked
WHY THIS MATTERS

Every "perishable" decision has the same two failure modes: overcapacity (you bought/made more than anyone wanted — waste) and undercapacity (you didn't have enough — lost revenue, or worse). The whole point of this model is to find the quantity that balances those two costs, not to eliminate either one. There is no order quantity that avoids both risks at once — accepting that is the first step to modeling it well.

Self-check: what makes a resource "perishable" in this sense?

It's not about spoilage in the literal sense — it's about time-boxing. Once the window closes (2pm for muffins, the appointment slot for an MRI, the shift for a nurse), any unused capacity is gone for good and any unmet demand is lost or delayed. Printer paper isn't perishable this way: unsold paper just waits for tomorrow's demand.

SPREADSHEET MODELING SKILL — NAME THE CELLS AND RANGES

Do not make the reader translate B5, B6, and B9 in their head

A cell address tells you where a value lives. A name tells you what the value means. That distinction is important in a managerial model because the formula itself should communicate the business logic.

Method 1 — Name Box (fastest for one cell or one range)

  1. Click the cell you want to name, for example B5.
  2. Click the Name Box immediately to the left of the Formula Bar.
  3. Type FixedCost.
  4. Press Enter. Pressing Enter is required to commit the name.
  5. Click another cell, then use the Name Box drop-down to return to FixedCost. Excel should select B5.

Method 2 — Formulas → Define Name / Name Manager

  1. Select the cell or range.
  2. Open Formulas → Define Name (or Name Manager → New).
  3. Enter a descriptive name and confirm the Refers to address.
  4. Use Name Manager later to audit, edit, or delete names.

SECTION 4 OF 10

The two lines that run the whole model

Everything downstream depends on two facts a manager already knows intuitively: you can't sell more muffins than you baked, and you can't sell more than customers actually want. In Excel, "the smaller of two numbers" is MIN() and "whatever's left over, but never negative" is MAX(0, …).

FORMULA-BY-FORMULA REASONING LAB

Every formula should tell a managerial story

Build the formula from the business rule, not from memorized cell addresses. Each formula below is on its own line and is followed by the reason it is written that way.

1. Full-price sales / Patients served
=MIN(Demand,QuantityBaked)
  • Demand is how many customers want the product.
  • QuantityBaked is how many units are available.
  • You cannot sell more than either limit, so MIN() returns the feasible number sold.
2. Leftovers / Unused doses
=MAX(0,QuantityBaked-Demand)
  • QuantityBaked-Demand calculates excess supply.
  • If demand is higher than supply, that subtraction becomes negative.
  • Negative leftovers are impossible, so MAX(0,...) floors the result at zero.
3. Lost sales / Unmet demand
=MAX(0,Demand-QuantityBaked)
  • Demand-QuantityBaked measures demand that could not be served.
  • If supply exceeds demand, the subtraction becomes negative.
  • MAX(0,...) prevents an impossible negative lost-sales value.
4. Total revenue
=(FullPriceSales*FullPrice)+(Leftovers*SalvagePrice)
  • Units sold normally earn FullPrice.
  • Excess units recover only SalvagePrice.
  • The two revenue streams must be calculated separately because their unit values differ, then added.
5. Total cost
=FixedCost+(QuantityBaked*UnitCost)
  • FixedCost occurs once regardless of quantity.
  • QuantityBaked*UnitCost is the variable cost that grows with the decision quantity.
  • Adding them gives total modeled operating cost.
6. Daily profit
=TotalRevenue-TotalCost
  • Profit is the residual after modeled costs are removed from modeled revenue.
  • This formula is intentionally simple because all detailed logic has already been built into Revenue and Cost.
7. Expected profit
=SUMPRODUCT(DemandProbability,ScenarioProfit)
  • DemandProbability contains the probability of each demand scenario.
  • ScenarioProfit contains the profit produced by that same scenario.
  • SUMPRODUCT multiplies each matched pair and adds the weighted results—the probability-weighted average profit.
8. Cost of underage (Cu)
=FullPrice-UnitCost
  • If you are one unit short, you lose the contribution that unit could have earned.
  • The contribution is selling value minus the cost you would have incurred to provide the unit.
9. Cost of overage (Co)
=UnitCost-SalvagePrice
  • If you have one unit too many, you paid UnitCost but recover only SalvagePrice.
  • The difference is the economic penalty of overage.
10. Critical ratio
=CostUnderage/(CostUnderage+CostOverage)
  • The denominator represents the combined consequences of being one unit too low or too high.
  • The numerator gives the underage cost its share of that total.
  • A higher underage penalty therefore pushes the target service percentile—and usually the recommended quantity—upward.
11. Absolute-reference version when named ranges are not used
=$B$5+($B$9*$B$6)
  • The dollar signs lock both row and column when the formula is copied.
  • Use this when you must work with addresses, but prefer named cells in a teaching/managerial model because the names preserve meaning.

Why profit doesn't keep climbing forever

Now hold the baking quantity fixed — the assignment asks you to fix Q = 180 — and vary only demand. Slide D from a slow morning to a rush, and watch what the profit line actually does.

SCENARIO ANALYSISQ held at 180
Demand (D)180
WHY THIS MATTERS — THE ASSIGNMENT'S OBSERVATION QUESTION

The line rises while D ≤ Q, because every extra customer buys one more full-price muffin. The instant D passes Q, the line goes flat — not down, flat — because there is nothing left to sell no matter how many more customers show up. Revenue is capped at Q × p, full stop. That flat ceiling is the cost of underbaking: every customer beyond Q is a sale you'll never recover, even though your reported profit isn't falling.

Self-check: which of D=140 or D=220 gets the higher profit at Q=180 — and why isn't the gap bigger?

D=220 wins ($374 vs $274) — more customers is still better up to the point Q is exceeded, since 40 fewer muffins going to the $1.00 discount bin is worth more than the sales you'd have gained. But the gap is capped: no matter how far D climbs past 180, profit stays at $374, because 180 is the most you baked.

Turning 100 days of data into a probability

So far D has been a single guess. In reality you have history: 100 past days of muffin demand, some slow, some busy. A pivot table turns that list into the "how often does each demand level happen" table the optimization step needs.

SAMPLE OF THE RAW LOGillustrative — your CSV has 100 rows

Grouped into bins of 10 and expressed as a share of all 100 days, that log turns into the probability table your assignment gives you for the optimization step:

BUILD THIS NEXT, IN EXCEL

Insert a Pivot Table from the CSV → drag Actual_Muffin_Demand to Rows → right-click a value → Group… in steps of 10 → drag Actual_Muffin_Demand to Values → change the value field setting to Show Values As → % of Column Total. Those percentages are your demand probabilities.

Self-check: why group into bins of 10 instead of using every exact value?

With continuous-ish demand, almost every exact value (161, 162, 163…) shows up once or not at all across 100 days, which gives you a flat, useless "probability" of 1% each. Grouping into bins gives you enough days per bin to see a real pattern — while still being fine-grained enough to distinguish a slow day from a rush.

SECTION 8 OF 10

The formula that skips the trial and error

Every unit you're short of demand costs you the margin you would have earned — that's the cost of underage (Cu). Every unit you have left over costs you the difference between what you paid and what you salvaged — the cost of overage (Co). Their ratio tells you exactly what percentile of the demand distribution to bake for, with no trial and error at all.

Self-check: at the assignment's prices, the critical ratio is 0.92 — what does that mean in plain English?

It means the ideal bake quantity is high enough that demand falls at or below it 92% of the time — because understocking a $2.30-margin muffin hurts a lot more than overstocking a muffin that still salvages $1.00. With prices this lopsided, the model deliberately accepts frequent, small leftovers to avoid rare, expensive stockouts.

SECTION 10 OF 10

The same math, higher stakes

Swap the numbers and the exact same five formulas run a flu clinic deciding how many vaccine doses to order. What changes is not the model — it's what "leftover" and "stockout" actually cost.

MORNING BREW CAFÉsame five formulas
ETHICAL VS. FINANCIAL — TASK 2 OF THE ASSIGNMENT

Order 170 doses; 200 patients show up. The spreadsheet reports a clean financial profit — revenue is simply capped at 170 sales, exactly like the muffin model caps at Q. But 30 people who wanted a flu shot didn't get one. Net Profit and Public Health point to different order quantities here: maximizing Cu (the true social cost of a missed vaccination — clinic visits, spread, lost workdays) would push Q well above what maximizes revenue alone.

A NON-PRICE LEVER FOR THE CAFÉ

Bundle day-old muffins into a discounted "yesterday's bake" box, or take pre-orders for the next morning — both shrink the leftover pile without touching price on fresh stock.

A NON-PRICE LEVER FOR THE CLINIC

Cross-train nurses so a quiet department can "float" staff to a busy one, or route overflow patients to telehealth — both absorb a demand spike without holding idle capacity on standby all season.

Self-check: is an empty hospital bed or a patient waiting in the hallway the more expensive kind of "wrong"?

There's no single correct number — that's the point of the question. An empty bed has a clear, bounded financial cost (lost reimbursement for one night). A patient boarding in a hallway has a cost that's harder to price: worse outcomes, staff strain, and reputational damage that can suppress future demand in ways a one-day profit model never captures. Naming that asymmetry out loud is more important than picking a number for it.

Self-check: a 4-week MRI wait doesn't show up anywhere in this profit formula — where would you add it?

It would show up as an inflated Cu: the true cost of underage isn't just today's missed appointment, it's the reputational and clinical cost of a growing waitlist. Modeling it honestly usually means treating Cu as larger than the raw margin suggests, which pushes the critical ratio — and the recommended capacity — higher.
