2. Probability — the language of uncertainty

A manager cannot eliminate uncertainty, but can express it numerically. If an emergency room has a 15% chance of a surge on any given Friday, imagine 100 comparable Fridays: about 15 would have a surge and about 85 would not. Probability describes what may happen, not what must happen on any particular Friday.

P(no surge) = 1 − P(surge) = 1 − 0.15 = 0.85 = 85%

Independence is a testable assumption

Two Fridays are independent if knowing the first had a surge does not change your estimate for the second. Only under independence can we multiply 0.15 × 0.15 to get a 2.25% chance of both having surges. Do not confuse this with mutually exclusive, which means two outcomes cannot occur together.

From one Friday to a month

  1. We want at least one surge in four Fridays: one or more.
  2. It is easier to find the opposite event: no surge on all four Fridays.
  3. Assuming independence, multiply four no-surge probabilities: 0.85⁴.
  4. Subtract from one: 1 − 0.85⁴ ≈ 47.8%.
Why season matters: suppose half the months have a 25% surge chance per Friday and half have 5%. The daily average still equals 15%, but the monthly probability of at least one surge is roughly 68.4% in flu months and only 18.5% in quiet months. Each month's Fridays may be independent conditional on month type, while Fridays across the mixture are not independent. The resulting overall monthly risk is approximately 43.5%, not 47.8%.
One Friday surge chance15.0%
At least one in four47.8%
No surges all month52.2%

Manager's lens: If the manager controls staffing over a month, the monthly chance of a surge and seasonality matter more than a single Friday's 15% risk. An on-call roster can be more defensible than hoping the risk never materializes.

First-principles deep dive

Start from first principles: What does probability count?

Imagine recording whether the emergency department experiences a surge every Friday for many years. Each Friday is one trial; a surge is an event. If 15 of 100 comparable Fridays had a surge, the observed relative frequency is 15/100 = 0.15. With enough relevant records, relative frequency can inform an estimate of future risk. It is an estimate, not a promise about the next Friday.

Probability = favorable outcomes / equally likely possible outcomes (simple counting setting)

That simple ratio works for equally likely possibilities, such as a fair six-sided die: P(rolling 5)=1/6. For hospital data, we use observed frequencies or statistical models instead; not all Fridays are equally likely to be busy.

Build the rules, one at a time

  1. Bounds: 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1. A probability of 125% cannot be valid.
  2. Exhaustive alternatives: Surge and no surge cover all possibilities, so P(surge)+P(no surge)=1.
  3. Complement: Rearranging the previous equation gives P(no surge)=1−P(surge).
  4. OR rule: If two types of disruption can overlap, add their probabilities and subtract the double-counted overlap: P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B).
  5. AND rule: P(A and B)=P(A)P(B|A). If A and B are independent, P(B|A)=P(B), so multiplication simplifies.
Worked OR example. P(surge)=.15, P(equipment failure)=.10, and P(both)=.03. We calculate .15+.10−.03=.22, or 22%. Adding .15+.10 without subtracting the overlap incorrectly counts those three jointly disrupted nights twice.

Conditional probability: why a denominator changes

Imagine 1,000 product launches. In 600 cases, a competitor also launches. Of those 600, 270 succeed. Without a competitor, 328 of 400 succeed. When the question says given that a competitor launches, we restrict the denominator to the 600 launches in that row—not all 1,000.

SituationSuccessFailureTotal
Competitor launches270330600
No competitor32872400
Total5984021,000
P(success | competitor) = 270/600 = 45%

But P(competitor | failure)=330/402≈82.1%. Those probabilities answer different questions. Reversing the condition changes which cases belong in the denominator.

Why an average can mislead: conditional independence

Suppose the month itself is chosen at random: half are flu months, half quiet. The probability of a surge on a randomly picked Friday is 0.5(.25)+0.5(.05)=.15. But once the month type is fixed, several Fridays share that same underlying risk. The unconditional probability of a four-Friday surge cannot simply be calculated by treating all Fridays as independent with p=.15.

  1. Flu months: 1−(.75)^4=.6836 or 68.36%.
  2. Quiet months: 1−(.95)^4=.1855 or 18.55%.
  3. Average across types: .5(.6836)+.5(.1855)=.4345 or 43.45%.
  4. Compare with the incorrect unconditional-independence shortcut: 1−(.85)^4=.4780 or 47.80%.

Decision point: A hospital supervisor schedules actual people, not average probabilities. Staffing plans should reflect high-risk seasons and plausible surge clusters.

1.1 Probability begins with counting

Real-world situation. Hospital admissions show 15 unusually busy Friday nights out of 100 comparable Fridays. Before a formula, identify the experiment (a Friday), event (surge), and total number of opportunities (100).

Start from first principles

Probability is favorable outcomes divided by equally relevant opportunities; historical frequencies approximate future risks only when periods are sufficiently comparable.

P(surge) = 15/100 = 0.15 = 15%; P(no surge) = (100-15)/100 = 0.85. The two exhaustive outcomes sum to 1.
What a manager should do

Build the roster for a whole planning period, not merely a typical Friday.

Common misconception

A 15% risk does not mean precisely one surge every 6.67 Fridays; clustering and changing conditions can disrupt the average.

1.2 Why addition subtracts overlap

Real-world situation. Some Friday nights have a patient surge (15%), some have equipment failure (10%), and 3% have both. The director wants the chance of at least one disruption.

Start from first principles

Adding 15% and 10% counts the nights with BOTH problems twice. Set logic explains the subtraction: union = A + B - intersection.

P(surge OR failure) = 0.15+0.10-0.03 = 0.22 = 22%. These events are not mutually exclusive since the overlap is 3%.
What a manager should do

Plan escalation protocols for the combined disruption risk rather than simply summing alarm probabilities.

Common misconception

Adding probabilities without subtracting overlap overstates the disruption chance.

1.3 Independent is not mutually exclusive

Real-world situation. A hospital can have a surge and an equipment fault simultaneously, whereas a flu season cannot be both high-volume and mild when those are exclusive classifications.

Start from first principles

Mutually exclusive means the joint chance is zero. Independent means observing A does not revise the chance of B; P(B|A)=P(B). Positive-probability mutually exclusive events are necessarily dependent.

Independent Fridays: P(surge twice)=0.15×0.15=0.0225. Exclusive seasonal labels: P(high AND mild)=0.
What a manager should do

Choose the multiplication rule only after reviewing the assumptions about common causes.

Common misconception

Different words describe different structures; zero overlap does not prove independence.

1.4 Why a small risk becomes large over time

Real-world situation. Staffing happens across four Fridays, but the 15% statistic describes just one Friday.

Start from first principles

At least one event is easier calculated as 1 minus the chance of zero events. The exponent represents multiplying independent no-event probabilities across periods.

P(at least one) = 1-(1-0.15)^4 = 1-0.52200625 = 0.47799375 ≈ 47.8%.
What a manager should do

A nearly one-in-two monthly chance justifies examining standby nurse coverage.

Common misconception

Multiplying four times 15% to get 60% double-counts multiple-surge months.

1.5 Seasonal dependence changes the calculation

Real-world situation. In half of all months, influenza conditions raise each Friday risk to 25%; in the other half, risk is 5%.

Start from first principles

Conditional independence within month type is not the same as unconditional independence across a mixture of high-risk and low-risk months. The mean Friday risk remains 15%, yet monthly risk differs.

P(≥1|flu)=1-0.75^4=68.36%; P(≥1|quiet)=1-0.95^4=18.55%; mixture=0.5×68.36%+0.5×18.55%=43.45%.
What a manager should do

Use seasonal rosters and forecasts, not the 15% headline alone.

Common misconception

The overall 47.8% calculation requires stronger independence assumptions and is not identical to the seasonal-mixture calculation.

1.6 Conditional probability from a frequency table

Real-world situation. Imagine 1,000 product launches. Competitors enter in 600; of those, 270 succeed. In the other 400, 328 succeed.

Start from first principles

The phrase given a competitor means restrict your denominator to competitor cases, not all launches or all successes.

P(success|competitor)=270/600=45%; P(competitor|failure)=330/402≈82.1%; P(success)=270/1000+328/1000=59.8%.
What a manager should do

A competitor forecast should update launch probabilities before pricing and investment are approved.

Common misconception

P(A|B) and P(B|A) are generally different; confusing their denominators changes the managerial conclusion.

3. Expected monetary value: Is the potential gain worth the risk?

Think of a company considering a new product. Market research estimates a 60% chance of earning $100,000 and a 40% chance of losing $30,000. The decision is not simply whether success is more likely: we must account for the sizes of the gains and losses.

  1. Multiply the success probability by the gain: 0.60 × $100,000 = $60,000.
  2. Multiply the failure probability by the loss: 0.40 × −$30,000 = −$12,000.
  3. Add them: $60,000 − $12,000 = $48,000 expected monetary value.
  4. Compare with declining the launch ($0), but explicitly assess whether a $30,000 loss is survivable.
EMV = ∑ [probability of outcome × payoff of outcome]
EMV is not a prediction that the next launch will earn $48,000. In this simple model it will either gain $100,000 or lose $30,000. The average is useful across repeated comparable decisions, while a one-time bet must also be judged by downside and risk tolerance.
Expected monetary value$48,000
Decision by EMVLaunch
Break-even success probability23.08%

How much uncertainty can we tolerate?

Standard deviation measures the spread around the expected payoff. The product launch has an SD of approximately $63,700. A city sensor project may have a comparable EMV ($50,000) but a much larger SD ($150,000), so a public manager could reasonably prefer the less volatile choice. Break-even analysis solves for the success probability that makes EMV equal zero: p = 30,000 / 130,000 = 23.08%.

Certainty equivalent

Suppose a manager can accept $45,000 guaranteed or a 50–50 gamble paying $100,000 or $0. The gamble's EMV is $50,000, but the manager may prefer certainty. If $45,000 is the amount they judge equivalent to the gamble, their risk premium is $50,000 − $45,000 = $5,000.

First-principles deep dive

Derive expected value from actual repeated decisions

Imagine 100 similar product launches under the stated probabilities: about 60 would succeed and 40 would fail. Total successful payoff would be 60×$100,000=$6,000,000. Total failure losses would be 40×−$30,000=−$1,200,000. Net is $4,800,000, so the average per launch is $4,800,000/100=$48,000. Dividing the counts by 100 produces the familiar probability-weighted formula.

EMV = Σ [probability of outcome × payoff of outcome]
OutcomeProbabilityPayoffContribution to EMV
Successful launch0.60+$100,000+$60,000
Failed launch0.40−$30,000−$12,000
Total1.00—$48,000

Why two options with similar averages can feel very different

Expected monetary value tells us the center. Standard deviation tells us the spread. First subtract the mean from each payoff, square those differences so positive and negative distances cannot cancel, weight by probabilities, then take the square root to return to dollar units.

  1. Success deviation: $100,000−$48,000=$52,000.
  2. Failure deviation: −$30,000−$48,000=−$78,000.
  3. Weighted squared deviations: .60×52,000²=$1,622,400,000 and .40×78,000²=$2,433,600,000.
  4. Variance=$4,056,000,000 dollars². Standard deviation=√4,056,000,000≈$63,687.

Variance is in squared dollars, so it is not naturally interpretable as money. Its square root, standard deviation, is again in dollars. A wider distribution indicates more variation around the average, not necessarily a worse project.

Derive the break-even chance of success

Call the unknown success probability p. Because success and failure exhaust the possibilities, failure has probability (1−p).

EMV(p) = 100,000p − 30,000(1−p) = 130,000p − 30,000
  1. At break-even, EMV=0.
  2. So 130,000p−30,000=0.
  3. Add 30,000 to each side: 130,000p=30,000.
  4. Divide: p=30,000/130,000≈0.2308, or 23.08%.

A 60% estimated success chance is above that threshold, but estimates can change. This comparison is called sensitivity analysis: would a reasonable change to the input reverse the decision?

Certainty equivalent: what is the gamble worth to this manager?

Imagine a sure $45,000 versus a 50/50 gamble paying either $100,000 or $0. The gamble has EMV=$50,000. Someone who feels indifferent between the gamble and guaranteed $45,000 has a certainty equivalent of $45,000. Their risk premium equals $50,000−$45,000=$5,000, expressing the expected amount they would surrender to avoid uncertainty.

Do not confuse: The certainty equivalent is an assessed preference, not an amount obtained by plugging into the EMV formula alone.

2.1 Derive expected monetary value from 100 repeated decisions

Real-world situation. A business considers 100 comparable launches: 60 winners yield $100,000 each and 40 failures cost $30,000 each.

Start from first principles

Count the total dollars first. Probability weights simply replace the frequencies from a large repeated group.

60×$100,000+40×(-$30,000)=$4,800,000; divide by 100 launches=$48,000 per launch. Therefore EMV=0.6×$100,000+0.4×(-$30,000).
What a manager should do

EMV helps compare repeated tolerable bets; it does not show whether a single loss could be fatal.

Common misconception

$48,000 is neither guaranteed nor one of the two individual possible payoffs.

2.2 Where standard deviation comes from

Real-world situation. Two options can have a $50,000 average but very different worst cases. A spread statistic makes the difference measurable.

Start from first principles

Take each payoff minus the EMV; square that distance to prevent positive and negative deviations from cancelling; weight by probability; take the square root to return to dollars.

Launch: +$100k−$48k=$52k and −$30k−$48k=−$78k. Variance=.6×52,000²+.4×78,000²=$4,056,000,000; SD≈$63,687.
What a manager should do

Compare risk and financial capacity alongside average reward.

Common misconception

Variance is in squared dollars; standard deviation restores dollar units. A high SD is not automatically a negative EMV.

2.3 Solve the break-even success probability

Real-world situation. Marketing estimates a 60% success chance, but the estimate may be wrong. What is the minimum acceptable success chance?

Start from first principles

Let p denote success, so 1−p is failure. Set weighted gain plus weighted loss equal to zero and solve for p.

100,000p−30,000(1−p)=0 → 130,000p=30,000 → p=23.08%.
What a manager should do

The gap between forecast 60% and threshold 23.08% is a margin for model error, not a guarantee.

Common misconception

A break-even probability is not the probability that the project actually succeeds.

2.4 Sure money, risk premium, and certainty equivalent

Real-world situation. A manager can receive $45,000 for sure or accept a 50-50 gamble yielding $100,000 or $0.

Start from first principles

The certainty equivalent is the guaranteed amount judged equally appealing as the gamble. Risk premium is EMV minus that amount. Different decision-makers can assign different certainty equivalents.

EMV(gamble)=0.5×$100,000+0.5×$0=$50,000; if CE=$45,000, risk premium=$5,000. CE<EMV implies risk aversion in this example.
What a manager should do

State whether management is accepting or avoiding risk and why.

Common misconception

Do not treat a stated CE as calculated purely from probabilities—it comes from preferences.

4. Is better information worth buying?

Business leaders order market studies, hospital leaders order diagnostic tests, and city leaders run pilots because being wrong can be expensive. But buying information is itself a decision: a $30,000 survey is not worth purchasing when it could improve expected outcomes by only $12,000.

Perfect information, step by step

  1. Without new information, the product launch has EMV of $48,000.
  2. With a perfect forecast, launch only when success is known; do not launch when failure is known.
  3. Expected payoff with perfect information: 0.6 × $100,000 + 0.4 × $0 = $60,000.
  4. EVPI = $60,000 − $48,000 = $12,000 maximum value.
EVPI = Best EV with perfect information − Best EV without it

Imperfect information and Bayes' rule

Suppose a pilot identifies whether new streetlight sensors work correctly 90% of the time. Among 1,000 situations, the technology works in 500. Of those, 450 pilots pass; in the 500 cases where technology fails, 50 pilots incorrectly pass. Therefore 500 pilots pass, and 450 of those correspond to working technology.

P(works | pilot passes) = 450 / (450 + 50) = 90%

After a passing pilot, installing the system has expected net savings 0.9($200,000) + 0.1(−$100,000) = $170,000. After a failing pilot, expected value from installing is negative, so the city does not install. With passes and failures each occurring 50% of the time, EV with pilot is 0.5($170,000) + 0.5($0) = $85,000; EV without pilot = $50,000. EVSI = $35,000, before pilot cost.

Manager's lens: Information adds value if it could change your decision. The most a city should pay for this imperfect pilot is $35,000 on expected-value grounds; $50,000 is the upper bound even for perfect information.

First-principles deep dive

First ask: can information change the decision?

Information costs time and money. A survey cannot be worth much if we would take the same action regardless of its result. Its economic value comes from being able to avoid a bad decision or select a better one when evidence arrives.

Derive perfect-information value (EVPI)

  1. Without a forecast: launch EMV=$48,000 versus not launch=$0; choose launch.
  2. With a perfect forecast: if success will occur (60%), launch and receive $100,000. If failure will occur (40%), decline and receive $0.
  3. Average with perfect knowledge: .60($100,000)+.40($0)=$60,000.
  4. Extra expected value from knowledge: $60,000−$48,000=$12,000 EVPI.

Even a completely accurate market study priced at $15,000 is too expensive for this particular decision on expected monetary value grounds: it costs more than the $12,000 maximum benefit.

Understand Bayes' rule by counting people, not memorizing symbols

A city runs a streetlight-sensor pilot. Suppose 1,000 comparable projects: in 500 the technology works, in 500 it fails. The pilot identifies the outcome correctly 90% of the time in either condition. There are therefore 450 true passes, 50 false fails, 50 false passes, and 450 true fails.

RealityPilot passesPilot failsTotal
Technology works45050500
Technology fails50450500
Total5005001,000
  1. The pilot passed in 450+50=500 cases.
  2. Of those passing cases, 450 correspond to working technology.
  3. So P(works | passes)=450/500=.90.
  4. By the same logic, P(works | fails)=50/500=.10.
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Bayes' rule formalizes this update from a prior probability before testing to a posterior probability after the test. The numbers above depend on a 50% starting probability and the assumed test accuracy; another starting probability gives another posterior.

Work out sample-information value (EVSI)

After a pass, expected benefit of installing is .9($200,000)+.1(−$100,000)=$170,000. After a failure, it is .1($200,000)+.9(−$100,000)=−$70,000; a rational EMV decision is not to install, yielding $0.

  1. A pass and fail each have probability .5 under these example assumptions.
  2. Best strategy after seeing the signal has expected value .5($170,000)+.5($0)=$85,000.
  3. Best strategy without the pilot is install immediately, EMV=$50,000.
  4. EVSI=$85,000−$50,000=$35,000, before deducting pilot cost.
  5. Perfect-information value in this city case is $50,000, so the pilot captures 35,000/50,000=70% of the perfect-information benefit.
Manager's lens: Pay for a pilot only if its total expected benefit exceeds its full cost. An imperfect pilot cannot have greater value than perfect information under the same coherent decision assumptions.

3.1 Information only matters if it changes a decision

Real-world situation. A marketer offers a costly report predicting whether a new product will succeed. If the report never changes the launch decision, its decision value is zero.

Start from first principles

The value of information is incremental improvement from decisions made AFTER the information is known compared with the best decision beforehand.

Without research: best EMV=$48,000. With a perfect forecast: launch after success signal and skip after failure signal → 0.6×$100,000+0.4×$0=$60,000.
What a manager should do

Never approve a study solely because it is accurate; evaluate whether it changes actions and how much its result is worth.

Common misconception

Do not confuse total expected value with incremental value.

3.2 EVPI and regret: two ways to reach $12,000

Real-world situation. Without knowing the future, a launch can suffer a $30,000 failure. Perfect forecasts allow us to avoid those failures.

Start from first principles

EVPI is perfect-information expected payoff minus current best EMV. Regret is the loss relative to the best possible action had the state been known.

EVPI=$60,000−$48,000=$12,000. Equivalently, 40%×$30,000 avoided failure loss=$12,000 expected regret.
What a manager should do

Perfect information is an upper bound: a less-than-perfect survey cannot rationally be worth more under this model.

Common misconception

A supplier offering perfect predictions for $15,000 costs more than this $12,000 ceiling.

3.3 Bayes theorem begins with ordinary counts

Real-world situation. The city tests smart lights. In 1,000 trials: 500 devices work and 500 fail; among the working devices 450 pass, and among the failures 50 pass.

Start from first principles

A positive result is not proof. Focus on the 500 passing pilots: 450 truly work and 50 do not. Bayes is a systematic method for changing a prior probability when evidence arrives.

P(works|pass)=(450/1000)/(500/1000)=450/500=90%; P(works|fail)=50/500=10%.
What a manager should do

Run a pilot, then choose installation according to what a pass or fail implies for likely performance.

Common misconception

Do not confuse P(pass|works)=90% with P(works|pass); these match here because the specific prior is 50%.

3.4 EVSI: act after imperfect information

Real-world situation. The pilot passes in half of cases. After a pass, installation has expected value $170,000; after a fail, installation would average −$70,000.

Start from first principles

After seeing a pass the city installs; after a fail it does not. Multiply each signal probability by the best action available after that signal.

EV with pilot=.5×$170,000+.5×$0=$85,000. EVSI=$85,000−$50,000=$35,000. Efficiency=35,000/50,000=70%.
What a manager should do

Pay for the pilot only when its full cost is below its expected value and assumptions are credible.

Common misconception

An imperfect pilot still has value even though it never eliminates all risk.

5. Decision trees and expected utility

Decision trees organize choices and uncertainty. A square represents a decision under management control; a circle represents chance; end points describe gains and losses. We solve a tree from the right side toward the initial decision, averaging at chance nodes and selecting the best branch at choice nodes.

Hospital opens clinic → 70% gain $150,000 / 30% lose $50,000

Using money alone, EMV(open) = 0.70($150,000) + 0.30(−$50,000) = $90,000, versus $0 for not opening. But what if the hospital is running a deficit and cannot afford a loss? The same payoffs need not have equal subjective value to all organizations.

OutcomeDeficit hospital utilityLarge system utility
Gain $150,000+40+60
Lose $50,000−100−20
Do not open00

Deficit hospital: 0.7(40) + 0.3(−100) = −2 utils, so decline. Large system: 0.7(60) + 0.3(−20) = +36 utils, so open. This is not faulty mathematics: these organizations have different capacities to bear losses.

Manager's lens: An option can have positive EMV yet be inappropriate if its worst case threatens essential services, solvency or public trust.

First-principles deep dive

Construct a decision tree rather than guessing

A decision tree is a map of what management can choose and what uncertainty determines. Begin with the decision; draw a square for your choice to open or not open. From the open branch draw a circle for chance outcomes: 70% high volume and 30% mild season. Mark the financial payoffs at the ends. Not opening returns $0.

□ Open Express Flu Clinic?
↳ Open → ○ Chance → 70% high volume → +$150,000
　　　　　　↳ 30% mild season → −$50,000
↳ Don't open → $0

Fold back from the right

  1. At the chance circle, calculate .7($150,000)+.3(−$50,000)=$90,000.
  2. At the decision square, compare open=$90,000 with not open=$0.
  3. A risk-neutral manager using dollar EMV alone chooses to open.

Why would a hospital choose differently?

A $50,000 loss may lead to staff cuts in a financially distressed hospital, so its harm can exceed the simple dollar amount. Utility is a decision-maker's score of how acceptable each outcome is. Suppose management assigns the upside +40 utility units and the downside −100 utility units. This is a stated preference model, not a claim that utilities are literal dollars.

Expected utility(open) = .7(+40) + .3(−100) = 28−30 = −2 utils

Not opening provides 0 utils, which exceeds −2. Therefore the deficit hospital does not open, even though the expected monetary value is +$90,000. A well-funded hospital with different utility scores could reasonably choose open.

Derive the minimum acceptable winning probability

  1. Let p be the probability of high demand.
  2. Expected utility = 40p−100(1−p).
  3. At indifference, 40p−100+100p=0.
  4. 140p=100, so p=100/140≈71.43%.
  5. The estimated 70% falls just below the utility threshold, helping explain the cautious decision.
Important limitation: Risk attitudes are assessed from management's preferences, resources, and mission. Utility numbers should be justified rather than casually invented.

4.1 Build a decision tree from control and chance

Real-world situation. A CFO may open or not open a flu clinic. If opened, the clinic has a 70% high-demand outcome and a 30% mild-demand outcome.

Start from first principles

A square is a controllable decision. A circle is a chance event. Paths end at payoffs. Evaluate the branches from right to left: average at chance nodes and choose at decision nodes.

Open → 0.70($150,000)+0.30(−$50,000)=$90,000; do not open → $0. Under a dollar-only EMV objective, choose open.
What a manager should do

Make explicit which uncertainties are modeled and which decisions can actually be made after observing information.

Common misconception

You do not choose the profitable outcome—only whether to take the risky action.

4.2 Why identical dollars can produce opposite choices

Real-world situation. The deficit hospital and a large hospital face exactly the same $150,000 gain or $50,000 loss. But one loss could disrupt patient care while the other can be absorbed.

Start from first principles

What a manager should do

Rejecting a positive-EMV project can be rational when downside threatens the organization’s mission.

Common misconception

Do not assume utility values are obtained by converting each dollar at a common rate.

4.3 Derive different risk thresholds

These thresholds are specific to the utility scores in the guide, not universal hospital policy.

Where do simulated demand numbers come from?

7. How to interpret statistical evidence

Mean versus standard deviation

Mean measures the average outcome; standard deviation measures how dispersed outcomes are around the mean. In the practice model, high-demand periods average approximately $4,783 with SD $337, while low-demand periods average $2,136 with SD $1,333. Averages alone conceal this difference in predictability.

Standard error and sample size

Standard error estimates how much a sample average would change if we repeated sampling. Its basic approximation is SD ÷ √n. A larger sample can make an average more precise, even though individual period-to-period variability remains large.

SE(mean) = s / √n

p-value and hypothesis test

The null hypothesis H₀ usually says that two population averages are equal. A two-sample t-test looks at how large an observed difference is compared with expected sampling noise. The p-value is the chance, assuming H₀ and the test's assumptions hold, of seeing a result at least as extreme. When p < 0.05, analysts often reject H₀. A p-value is not the chance that H₀ is true and statistical significance does not guarantee practical importance.

95% confidence intervals

A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for the unknown population mean. The 95% refers to how often the method captures the true mean over hypothetical repeated samples, not a 95% chance that the next transaction falls inside the interval. The interval may be wide if outcomes vary greatly or sample sizes are small.

Correlation

Correlation (r) ranges from −1 to +1 and summarizes a linear relationship. In the inventory case, more demand generally leads to more profit, but correlation alone cannot establish causation. A shared factor or a built-in mathematical formula could produce the association.

Manager's lens: A defensible report answers: How much do we expect? What downside is plausible? How certain is the estimate? Is there evidence that two options differ? What could management change?

Standard deviation describes variation among individual outcomes. Standard error describes how much an estimated sample mean would vary across repeated independent samples. For n=150 and s=$1,642, SE=s/√n≈$134.1. Collecting four times as many observations approximately halves SE when the underlying spread stays similar.

Standard error of sample mean = s / √n

From evidence to a hypothesis test

Suppose a manager compares the mean outcomes of Strategy A and Strategy B. Start with the null hypothesis H₀: their true means are equal. The competing hypothesis is that their means differ. Differences in sample averages can arise from random sampling even if H₀ is true.

  1. Compute the observed difference in sample means: A−B.
  2. Estimate uncertainty in the difference: SE=√(sA²/nA + sB²/nB).
  3. Compute t=(meanA−meanB)/SE: a gap expressed in units of standard error.
  4. Use the appropriate t-distribution to determine a p-value: how surprising a result at least this extreme would be if H₀ were true.
  5. Compare with the preselected significance threshold, for example α=.05. A p-value below .05 is evidence against H₀, not proof that H₀ has 0% probability.
Business example from the guide: Mean A=$6,575.99, mean B=$9,099.14; sA=$1,651.28, sB=$7,141.96; nA=nB=150. Difference=−$2,523.15; SE≈$598.52; t≈−4.216, with p<.001. The data support a difference in average simulated profit, but a manager must still compare costs and downside risk.

What is a 95% confidence interval?

A confidence interval estimates uncertainty about the true mean. Its construction is sample mean ± (critical t multiplier × SE). For Business Scenario A: mean=$6,575.99, SE≈$134.83, multiplier≈1.976; margin≈$266.42. Thus the interval is roughly $6,309.57 to $6,842.41.

95% CI for mean ≈ sample mean ± t* × s/√n

A 95% procedure is designed so that 95% of intervals computed from repeated samples include the fixed true mean. The statement does not mean 95% of individual profits lie between those endpoints.

Correlation is not a cause-and-effect experiment

Suppose program overhead and net social impact have r=−.626. The negative sign means higher overhead tends to accompany lower impact; the magnitude indicates a moderately strong inverse linear association. It does not prove overhead caused lower impact. Both could respond to program size, geography, or other drivers.

r = Σ[(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)] / √[Σ(x−x̄)² × Σ(y−ȳ)²]

In the guide's five-program hand calculation, sums give numerator −34 and denominator √(40×30), so r≈−.98. A strong correlation may also be induced by equations built into the simulation model, which is why causal conclusions require a credible research design.

Read the charts like a manager

A histogram groups results into ranges and displays frequency; a box plot displays median, quartiles, and unusually distant points. Ask: where is the center? How wide is the spread? Does the distribution cross break-even? Which group has more extreme losses? The strongest recommendation combines this visual evidence with statistics rather than choosing by average alone.
